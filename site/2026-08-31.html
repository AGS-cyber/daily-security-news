The day's briefing leads with a China-linked espionage actor pivoting from hypervisors to the routers and authentication servers that run high-value networks — a move that lets the group steal credentials and blind the very logs defenders rely on. In the breach beat, McKesson confirms a massive claimed data theft and Berlin publicly refuses to pay a ransomware gang, while the patching queue demands attention for ServiceNow, a Ruby on Rails flaw already under active attack, and a Kaspersky exploit that just went public.

Research

The campaign tracked as Fire Ant has crossed a significant threshold. China-Linked Fire Ant Hijacks Cisco Routers to Steal Credentials and Blind Security Logs After years of focusing on VMware hypervisors, the actor is now compromising Cisco IOS XR routers, TACACS servers, and the Linux management hosts used to operate high-value networks. The TACACS angle is what makes this stand out: those servers hold the credentials used by network administrators to authenticate to network equipment, so a compromise there effectively hands the attacker a master key to the environment. Sygnia's investigation points to a long-running effort, and the added step of blinding security logs means defenders may not realize the extent of the intrusion until the actor wants them to. For organizations running IOS XR in service-provider or large enterprise backbones, this is not a routine endpoint incident; it is an attack on the infrastructure that everything else depends on.

The criminal ecosystem is also adopting new tools. Aurora ransomware operators have been observed using Cursor, an AI-powered coding assistant, during intrusions against at least ten targets. Aurora Ransomware Operators Use Cursor AI in Attacks Against 10 Targets The findings come from two independent analyses that examined infrastructure linked to the Russian-speaking group. The use of a legitimate coding assistant to help break into networks signals a practical shift toward AI-assisted cybercrime — not as a future possibility, but as something already happening. It lowers the expertise needed to write or adapt code, and it is the kind of development that security teams need to factor into their threat models for the rest of the year.

A different kind of trust abuse is on display in the ValleyRAT campaign. The Silver Fox actor is distributing the backdoor inside a signed Chinese adware application called QN Wallpaper. ValleyRAT Backdoor Hides in Signed Adware That Users Add to Antivirus Exclusions The adware is genuine, so the signed binary carries the trust of a legitimate product — and the campaign's success depends on users actively adding that trusted process to their antivirus exclusions. Once the exclusion is in place, ValleyRAT runs under a process that security tools are told to ignore. Kaspersky documented the campaign, which is a reminder that signed software and user naivety remain a reliable blend for malware delivery.

Breaches

McKesson has confirmed a data breach, and ShinyHunters is claiming the theft of 284 million records. McKesson Confirms Data Breach as Attacker Deadline Looms The number is enormous, and the looming deadline set by the attackers makes the situation more acute: they are applying extortion pressure to turn stolen data into payment before releasing it. For a healthcare company, the sensitivity of the data compound the impact. Even if no records are ever published, the confirmation will trigger notification obligations, investigative costs, and a reputational hit. The sheer scale of the claim, if accurate, would put this among the largest healthcare-related breaches on record.

Berlin is taking a different path through a similar crisis. The city administration has confirmed that Rhysida is attempting to extort it after stealing 5TB of government data and listing Berlin on its leak site. Berlin confirms data theft after Rhysida ransomware attack claims Berlin's refusal to pay sets a notable public-sector precedent, showing a major European capital willing to accept the consequences of a leak rather than fund extortion. That stance may deter future attacks, but it also means the city will soon face the public release of sensitive administrative material. Governments watching this case will be studying both the immediate fallout and whether the posture actually reduces future targeting.

Vulnerabilities

ServiceNow has patched three critical code injection vulnerabilities. ServiceNow Patches 3 Critical Code Injection Vulnerabilities The flaws could allow an attacker to execute arbitrary code and access or tamper with data in enterprise workflows. ServiceNow sits at the center of IT operations, HR processes, and business applications for many organizations, so the business impact of exploitation goes beyond a single server. Code injection in that context often leads to remote code execution, and the potential for data tampering makes the integrity of operational records a concern, not just confidentiality. The patches should be treated as urgent, and organizations should confirm they are running a fixed version.

The Ruby on Rails vulnerability known as KindaRails2Shell is already being attacked in the wild. Critical Ruby on Rails Vulnerability in Attackers’ Crosshairs This critical arbitrary file read flaw gives attackers the ability to extract secrets and, in many configurations, escalate to remote code execution. Active exploitation changes the math: this is no longer a theoretical vulnerability to patch in the next maintenance window. Rails powers a large portion of the web, and exposed applications are at immediate risk. The name KindaRails2Shell is itself a hint that the route to shell access is well understood. Patching should happen before the edges of the internet become the place where the flaw is discovered again.

The public release of a Kaspersky Endpoint Security exploit by Nightmare Eclipse adds another item to the urgent patch list. Nightmare Eclipse Drops ‘HardBreacher’ Kaspersky Product Exploit Kaspersky has already issued a patch for the vulnerability, which is good news, but the existence of a public exploit means unpatched installations are now a straightforward target. The exploit, dubbed "HardBreacher," will likely be integrated into tooling quickly. Organizations should not assume their Kaspersky Endpoint Security deployment is protected simply because a patch exists; they need to verify that the update has actually been applied across every endpoint. The gap between a patch being released and a patch being deployed has a name for a reason — and this is the moment that gap gets exploited.
