Breaches

McKesson, the healthcare and pharmaceutical distribution giant, has confirmed a cybersecurity incident involving unauthorized access to third-party applications and data theft. The extortion group ShinyHunters is publicly claiming that the stolen data includes 284 million patient records. If the claim is accurate, this would be one of the largest healthcare data exposures ever reported; if it is inflated, the incident still demonstrates that patient data remains a primary target for extortion-driven theft. The phrasing of the disclosure matters too: the compromise involved third-party applications, which means McKesson’s own defenses were not the only surface under attack. For healthcare organizations, this is another reminder that identity and access paths running through partners and vendors can create the same level of exposure as a direct breach, and that the boundary of responsibility no longer maps to the boundary of the network. Hasbro has also disclosed a data breach, confirming that employee personal information was exposed in connection with the cyberattack that disrupted its operations. The toy and game company is the latest example of a consumer-goods firm learning that HR systems are a first-class target, not an auxiliary one. Employee records are valuable because they support follow-on phishing, credential abuse, and identity fraud, and they often contain data on many more people than just the direct victim. The incident also highlights the supply-chain dimension of consumer-goods security: a breach at a household-name vendor can create obligations, investigations, and further exposures that ripple through partners sharing vendor management, procurement, or other sensitive workflows. Defenders in adjacent sectors should treat this as a reminder that workforce data needs the same monitoring and segmentation as customer data. Industry

Berlin’s state government has confirmed that it is the target of an extortion attempt following the compromise of the city’s state administrative network, and it has said publicly that it will not pay. The same statement disclosed that forensic work had found further data outflows in the Senate Department for Mobility, Transport, Climate Protection and Environment. The refusal is a policy signal as much as a security decision: once a major capital draws that line publicly, it may be harder for other public-sector victims to argue for a different course. It also reinforces a practical assumption for incident response — stolen administrative data should be treated as likely to be published, and containment and communication plans need to account for that from the start. The fact that forensics continue to surface additional data outflows is a reminder that scope determination for a network compromise is rarely a single event; it is an ongoing process that can change the risk picture weeks after the initial discovery. Brave’s latest release adds Email Aliases, which let users generate disposable email addresses when signing up to new services. The feature is a low-friction privacy control: it reduces cross-site tracking, limits the value of harvested addresses, and gives users a way to identify who reuses or sells their contact data. It is not a security boundary in the traditional sense — it will not stop malware or phishing — but it does make it easier for users to control how their email address propagates across the web. The limitation is structural rather than technical: this feature only helps people who run Brave, so its real-world impact is constrained by browser market share. For privacy-conscious users, it is a practical addition; as a systemic response to email-based tracking, it remains a niche tool. Brave browser adds email aliases to help users evade tracking

Research

Microsoft Threat Intelligence has published an analysis of a ClickFix campaign it calls TerminalFix. The chain begins with fake CAPTCHA prompts and moves through DLL sideloading to establish a reverse tunnel, with each stage designed to blend into normal system activity. The deeper lesson is the emphasis on the full intrusion sequence: defenders who monitor for a single indicator, such as a known payload or a suspicious process, can miss the middle stages where an attempted compromise turns into persistent access. The inclusion of detection and hunting guidance is useful for teams trying to build visibility across the entire chain rather than hunting one technique at a time. Campaigns like this also show that social engineering remains the most reliable initial access vector, even when the technical payload path is multistage and carefully built. New research from Unit 42 introduces a diagnostic called Perturbation Probing to measure how much perturbation an LLM safety mechanism can tolerate before it fails. The findings indicate that refusal behavior is concentrated in a thin neural layer, so small changes can flip a model from declining to complying. That is a concrete warning for security teams: model-level safety is too fragile to be the last line of defense. External, multi-layered controls — including input and output filtering, abuse monitoring, and least-privilege access for model-backed systems — remain the reliable baseline for production deployments. The research also suggests that evaluating an LLM for safety should include stress-testing its refusal behavior under perturbation, not just checking whether a model behaves on standard benchmarks.
