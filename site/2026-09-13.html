Actively exploited

The KEV expansion is the day's most actionable item because of what it covers rather than how many entries it carries. CISA Adds 5 Actively Exploited Artifactory, ScreenConnect, and RouterOS Flaws to KEV bundles five flaws across three products that sit in very different, very privileged places: an artifact repository, a remote-support tool, and edge routing. CISA's addition is predicated on evidence of exploitation in the wild, so treat all three as live rather than theoretical.

The one entry with full intelligence attached is CVE-2026-42016, an incorrect authorization flaw in JFrog Artifactory Self Hosted before 7.133.11. The mechanism is worth internalising: the token check validates the signature and issuer but not the token's scope, so a low-privileged authenticated user can escalate — CWE-863, the same category that keeps producing privilege-boundary collapses in CI/CD infrastructure. NVD scores it 8.8, which understates the practical risk when the affected system is the place your build artifacts, dependencies and deployment credentials live. KEV listing obliges U.S. federal civilian agencies to apply vendor mitigations in line with the relevant binding operational directive, and the catalog entry explicitly permits discontinuing use of the product where mitigations aren't available — a reminder that Artifactory's blast radius justifies a hard decision rather than a scheduling argument. Ransomware campaign use is recorded as unknown. For everyone outside the federal scope, the deadline is not yours, but the reason for the listing is: ScreenConnect and RouterOS have both been entry points before, and this batch says they still are.

Separately, Hackers exploit Tencent app flaw to deploy GrayRabbit malware reports a China-aligned espionage group exploiting a critical flaw in Tencent's Sogou Input Method for Windows to deploy the GrayRabbit backdoor. The vulnerability, CVE-2026-51990, is absent from KEV — a confirmed catalog miss, which says something about listing lag rather than about whether exploitation is happening, since the reporting describes it in the wild. Input methods are an unusually attractive target: they see every keystroke, they run with user context, and they update through their own channels, which makes them a supply-chain foothold that no amount of network segmentation touches. If you permit third-party input methods on managed Windows endpoints, that is now a decision you should be able to defend.

Breaches

Microsoft's disclosure in Attackers Use Passkey Phishing to Hijack Microsoft Cloud Accounts and Exfiltrate Data is a useful corrective to the assumption that passkeys close the account-takeover problem. The passkey-themed campaign does not defeat the cryptography; it targets the human and process layer around enrollment and recovery, then moves to cloud data exfiltration once inside. That is the pattern to instrument for: unexpected authenticator or passkey enrollment, recovery-method changes, and consent grants arriving shortly after a helpdesk interaction. The second campaign is a different abuse of trust — more than a million financial-fraud emails sent in a three-day window by impersonating executives, riding on third-party email delivery infrastructure so the messages carry legitimate sending reputation and authentication. Both cases share a lesson: the controls teams have invested in are working as designed, and the attackers are routing around them through channels that were never in scope.

Industry

Anthropic's Dario Amodei used Anthropic CEO Dario Amodei Says AI Industry Needs to Give Safety Measures Time to Catch Up to argue that the industry needs to give safety measures room to catch up, warning that within six to twelve months AI could direct a swarm of agents capable of seizing control of the internet. Read it as a governance argument from someone with a commercial stake in the outcome, but do not dismiss the operational half. Agentic tooling is arriving in enterprise environments with identities, credentials and API reach that most authorization models were never designed to bound. The questions worth asking now are unglamorous: what identity does an agent hold, what can it do without a human in the loop, and what does your audit trail actually record when it acts. Those answers are cheap to build today and expensive to retrofit.
