TeamCity Exploitation Leads a Day of Build-Server, CPU, and Database Attacks

8 of 125 stories written up · 12 of 12 sources · generated 20:15 UTC

Defenders woke up to an actively exploited build-server vulnerability, a guilty plea in the Snowflake extortions, and new research that erodes trusted CPU mitigations and turns database engines into living-off-the-land attack platforms. The through-line is the same: attackers keep finding ways to use what you already trust — build pipelines, AI browsers, routers, compilers, and SQL engines — against you.

Actively exploited

The U.S. cybersecurity agency has added a critical JetBrains TeamCity remote code execution flaw to its known exploited vulnerabilities catalog after confirming active exploitation. The bug, a deserialization issue in on-premise TeamCity servers, lets an unauthenticated attacker with network access take over a build server and, from there, likely the software supply chain that depends on it. TeamCity is a prime target because build servers hold credentials, signing keys, and source code; a foothold there is a springboard into production environments. The agency's move is the clearest signal yet that asset owners should treat this as an emergency patching event, not a routine update. CISA Flags TeamCity CVE-2026-63077 RCE Flaw Under Active Exploitation in the Wild

Industry

The Snowflake extortion saga reached a legal milestone. A 26-year-old Canadian man pleaded guilty to computer fraud and conspiracy for his role in hacking and extorting more than 165 organizations that used the cloud data storage provider, and to stealing call and text history records belonging to more than 100 million customers of a major U.S. carrier. The plea confirms what many suspected: the campaign was a coordinated, large-scale data theft operation, not a series of opportunistic intrusions. The guilty plea also puts a spotlight on the credential and identity failures that allowed the attackers to move so freely through victim environments. Canadian Man Pleads Guilty in Snowflake Extortions

Separately, a House committee report concludes that three Chinese telecommunications companies continue to hold significant footholds in the U.S. internet ecosystem despite their alleged connections to state-sponsored hacking campaigns. The report is the latest pressure point in the long-running policy fight over Chinese access to U.S. networks, and it lands at a moment when regulators are still deciding how much to restrict or unwind that presence. The concerns are not hypothetical: the same infrastructure that carries traffic can also be used to tap it, and the committee's findings will likely feed new legislative and regulatory proposals. Chinese telcos maintain deep US presence despite Salt Typhoon links, House committee says

Vulnerabilities

AI assistant browsers are the latest zero-click target. Researchers found that malicious emails or X posts can trigger a full hijack of the browser-based AI assistants in Claude and ChatGPT Atlas, with no user interaction beyond opening the message. The attacks reportedly remain unpatched after being disclosed, leaving users exposed to session takeover, credential theft, and malicious tool invocations. The finding is significant because AI assistants are increasingly granted broad permissions over email, files, and applications; a browser-level hijack bypasses the AI's own safeguards and gives the attacker the same trust boundary as the assistant. Until patches land, this is a vulnerability that defenders cannot fully mitigate with configuration changes. Zero-Click AI Browser Hacking: Claude and ChatGPT Atlas Hijacked via Emails, X Posts

On the hardware side, researchers disclosed a factory-shipped backdoor in at least 20 Chinese router models from Zbtlink. The implant is present across all 21 firmware images currently available from the vendor, spans more than two years of releases, and provides an unauthenticated root shell that starts automatically and attempts to beacon out to a remote server. For buyers, this is not a vulnerability waiting to be triggered — it is a deliberate, persistent backdoor at the supply-chain level. Any organization with these devices on their network should treat them as fully compromised and replace them, not just patch or configure them. Chinese-Made Zbtlink Routers Ship With Backdoor That Opens Unauthenticated Root Shells

Research

A new CPU attack punctures existing Spectre v2 mitigations. The technique, called INTERRUPT INJECTION, works by timing a hardware interrupt to land in the tiny gap between the processor sanitizing its branch predictor and the kernel reusing it. An unprivileged Linux program can use this to re-poison the predictor after the defense has run, defeating default mitigations on both Intel and AMD processors. The practical impact is still being measured, but the research matters because it shows that even carefully constructed microarchitectural defenses have a race window that attackers can slip into. Software-only workarounds may be possible, but CPU vendors are likely to need microcode-level fixes or new hardware designs. New Interrupt Injection Attack Can Bypass Spectre v2 Defenses on Intel and AMD CPUs

In a separate campaign, attackers turned an Oracle database into a fileless post-exploitation platform. The initial entry was a SQL injection flaw in a public-facing web application, but instead of dropping a webshell or executable, the attackers fed Java source code directly to the database, let Oracle compile it into stored schema objects, and ran commands from inside the database engine. The technique, tracked as khunt, ultimately gave them a path to Windows SYSTEM access without writing an executable to disk. For defenders, this is a reminder that SQL injection is no longer just a data-access problem; if the database can compile code, it becomes a foothold for operating-system compromise. Attackers Compile khunt Inside Oracle to Turn SQL Injection Into Windows SYSTEM Access

Breaches

Switzerland's federal IT office reports that attackers exploited vulnerabilities in its Microsoft SharePoint servers and compromised around 200 accounts. The breach, which officials believe may be state-sponsored espionage, underscores how common it is for a well-resourced attacker to find an unpatched on-premises collaboration server. SharePoint frequently holds an organization's crown jewels — documents, credentials, and internal communications — and 200 compromised accounts gives an intruder ample room to move laterally and extract intelligence. The incident is still in the investigation phase, but the pattern matches some of the more aggressive espionage operations seen recently against European government networks.
