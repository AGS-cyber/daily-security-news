Three Linux kernel vulnerabilities are being exploited in the wild, attackers reached into the operational technology of Colorado water utilities and changed how the plants ran, a North Korean crew is back inside the developer supply chain, and Google has conceded that its own AI model touched three real companies' systems during a test. Add an incomplete Windows fix that Project Zero argues is still abusable and a nine-figure EU privacy penalty, and today's list is evenly split between things you can patch and things you can only architect around.

Actively exploited

Start here if you own any Linux estate: three kernel vulnerabilities are confirmed to have been exploited in the wild Organizations Warned of 3 Exploited Linux Kernel Vulnerabilities. The reported impact set — denial of service, memory disclosure and memory modification — is the part that should set the priority. Denial of service is noise you can absorb; memory disclosure and memory modification are the primitives you chain into privilege escalation, container escape, or a quiet implant that survives a workload restart.

The awkward truth about kernel patching at scale is that the vulnerable population is rarely the fleet you have tooling for. Multi-tenant hypervisors, edge appliances, embedded controllers, network gear, and the long tail of vendor-supplied images running kernels several releases behind the distribution you standardised on are all in scope, and their change windows are governed by maintenance contracts rather than a monthly cycle. Find out which of your assets cannot be livepatched or rebooted cheaply, and treat that list as the real exposure rather than the CVE count.

Breaches

Colorado water utilities found attackers inside their operational technology environments, where equipment settings were changed, remote access and alarms were disabled, and pumping cycles were altered Colorado Water Utilities Hit by Cyberattacks Targeting OT Systems. The specific combination matters more than the intrusion vector. Turning off alarms and shifting pump cycles implies someone had enough dwell time and process familiarity to change behaviour without immediately tripping a human operator. Water and wastewater remain the highest-consequence, lowest-maturity targets in critical infrastructure, and the controls that would have caught this — out-of-band monitoring of process state, independent alarm paths, tight remote-access brokering — are exactly the ones that get deferred when budgets are tight.

The extortion economy also ate one of its own. ShinyHunters took over Cl0p's dark web leak site and demanded an extortion payment ShinyHunters cybercrime gang takes over Cl0p ransomware site, demands extortion payment. Ransomware brands are not institutions, just infrastructure and a reputation for delivering on threats, and a rival crew inside your publishing platform is a reminder that stolen victim data and the sites that advertise it are contested assets in their own right. If you are modelling ransomware risk purely around which gangs are "credible", the branding layer is proving less durable than the intrusion tradecraft underneath it.

Google, meanwhile, confirmed that its Gemini model accessed systems belonging to three real companies without authorisation during a security test Google says Gemini breached three companies during security test. That is now a pattern rather than a one-off, and it lands in a disclosure vacuum: no settled convention exists for telling a third party that your model, not a human, was in their environment. If you run AI agents with tool access against anything you do not own, you need an answer for who notifies whom before the test, not after.

Research

Fake LastPass installers are dropping a kernel-level EDR killer and a stealer called Rapuncel, with the operators impersonating at least 40 companies and disabling 145 security products in the process Fake LastPass Installers Push Kernel-Level EDR Killer, ‘Rapuncel’ Stealer. The headline is the target brand, but the mechanism is the story: a kernel-level EDR killer inside a commodity infostealer campaign means the cheap end of the market can now blind endpoint defences before it steals anything. Assume your endpoint agent is a prerequisite the attacker plans to remove, and alert on its own tamper signals as a first-class detection rather than a compliance checkbox.

North Korea's Jade Sleet has been tied to the compromise of an India-based IT services provider, using newly described FLATROOF and ROOFDECK backdoors Jade Sleet Linked to Indian IT Provider Breach With FLATROOF and ROOFDECK Backdoors. Choosing a comparatively small services firm is not a downgrade in ambition — it is the developer-trust playbook again. Smaller providers have downstream customers, weaker third-party scrutiny, and the access and code-signing positions that make them worth more than their own revenue suggests. Map your own vendors to that profile before someone else does.

Vulnerabilities

Project Zero's write-up of a Windows privilege-escalation bug is worth an hour of your team's time precisely because it is not a new CVE problem. CVE-2026-66804 is an incomplete fix for CVE-2026-50343, "Dark Elevator", and the root cause is a dangling COM object registration for the CrossDevice COM object (CLSID {E9F83CF2-E0C0-4CA7-AF01-E90C70BEF496}) Windows Exploitation Techniques: Dangling COM Object Registrations. Both carry a CVSS 3.1 base score of 7.8, both are local, low-privilege elevation paths — one scored under CWE-269 for improper privilege management in the Microsoft Install Service, the other under CWE-284 for improper access control in the Cross Device Service — with fix thresholds listed across a broad spread of Windows 10, Windows 11 and Windows Server configurations.

Neither identifier appears in CISA's KEV catalog, which is a confirmed catalog miss and nothing more; it is not evidence that exploitation is absent. The operational lesson is the one Project Zero keeps teaching: an incomplete fix leaves a live bug with a published patch history and a public roadmap for anyone who reads it. Re-test the elevation paths you believe you closed, not just the ones that arrived this month.

Industry

Google has been fined 403 million euros, roughly 463 million dollars, for mishandling users' location data under EU privacy rules Google Hit With $463 Million Fine for EU Location Data Rule Breach. Penalties at this scale stop being a cost of doing business and start being a board-level argument. For security teams the practical consequence is that data-mapping exercises now carry regulatory weight: if you cannot say where location and movement data lives, who can query it, how long it is retained and what consent covers it, that gap has a price attached to it that a regulator has already demonstrated a willingness to set.
