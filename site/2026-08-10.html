Three separate active-exploitation warnings demand immediate patching, alongside state-linked infrastructure intrusions, a logistics supply-chain breach, passkey research that tempers the promise of phishing-resistant MFA, critical identity software flaws, and an AI model that prompted its own developer to pause.

Actively exploited

The lead is a critical command injection flaw in Progress Kemp LoadMaster, now flagged by CISA as actively exploited. LoadMaster commonly fronts enterprise applications, so this is not a niche bug: an attacker with command execution can pivot into adjacent networks, capture traffic, and plant persistence on the appliance itself. The flaw has been added to the known exploited vulnerabilities catalog, making it a remediation priority for federal agencies and a clear signal for everyone else. For most organizations, the more urgent step is asset discovery — many do not know every LoadMaster instance they run. Defenders should patch immediately and, because exploitation is already underway, hunt for post-exploitation activity before assuming the fix closes the incident. Critical Progress LoadMaster flaw now actively exploited in attacks

TrueConf Server is the second active front. The threat actor Head Mare is chaining unpatched TrueConf flaws to replace client installers with the PhantomCore backdoor, in a campaign documented against Russian firms across instrumentation, electronics, transport, energy, IT, and software development. The installer replacement is the key detail: victims need only fetch a legitimate-looking client update to receive the backdoor, without touching a malicious server. The campaign’s broad sector spread suggests a general-purpose backdoor capability rather than a single vertical target. Head Mare has abused TrueConf before, so unpatched instances are a repeatable target. Remove them from the network or update them now, and review client installation history for signs of compromise. TrueConf Server Flaws Exploited to Replace Client Installers with PhantomCore

Metabase completes the trio. The vendor has patched a zero-day that lets unauthenticated remote attackers gain administrative access to instances. The flaw requires no credentials and hands over the analytics platform and its data, making it ideal for broad scanning and follow-on ransomware. Exploit activity is already public, so self-hosted deployments should treat the update as an emergency. Any internet-facing instance should be considered probed; after patching, check for unexpected admin accounts and unusual API or query activity. Metabase Patches Vulnerability Exploited as Zero-Day

Research

Iran-linked hackers have targeted industrial control systems at water facilities in at least a dozen U.S. states, with New Jersey and Alabama the latest identified. The intrusions appear designed for persistent access rather than immediate disruption, though that is little comfort for a sector where availability and safety dominate. The breadth of targeting suggests a deliberate campaign against critical infrastructure, not opportunistic scanning. Utilities should audit internet-exposed HMIs, remote access tools, and the credentials that could grant lateral movement into OT environments. New Jersey, Alabama Join States Targeted in Water Cyberattacks

Three independent research efforts also demonstrated attacks against passkeys that complicate the promise of phishing-resistant MFA. The attacks do not break the cryptography; they recover synced private keys exposed by Windows, abuse a cloud-synced passkey system from malware on the victim machine, and reuse signed authentication material. Passkeys remain stronger than reusable passwords, but endpoint compromise defeats them just as it defeats traditional credentials. Enterprises rolling out passkeys should not treat them as a substitute for endpoint detection, and software-synced passkey implementations warrant a different risk assessment than hardware-anchored ones. New Passkey Attacks Can Recover Synced Private Keys or Bypass Phishing-Resistant MFA

Breaches

Valve is notifying Steam hardware customers in Europe that their data was stolen through its shipping partner, CEVA Logistics. The stolen data — names, addresses, and order details — is ideal for targeted phishing that references a real hardware order, which is exactly what makes such messages credible. The incident is also a reminder that breach exposure is often defined by the weakest partner in the fulfillment chain, and that data minimization matters when sharing customer information with logistics vendors. Valve notifies Steam hardware customers of a data breach

Vulnerabilities

Critical flaws in the software behind Belgium’s electronic ID system affect an installed base of about two million people, including eight of Belgium’s ten largest banks and more than sixty government agencies. These are not ordinary product bugs: a flaw in eID middleware is a flaw in the identity trust fabric used for authentication and identity verification across financial and public services. The concentration of trust in a single software stack makes the impact disproportionate. Users should update, and relying parties should audit logs for signs of misuse before the patch was applied. Critical Flaws Discovered in Belgian eID Software Used by 2 Million People

Industry

OpenAI has paused some internal work on its Astra model after an internal evaluation found significant advances in agentic coding and cybersecurity. The company is implementing security controls for higher-capability models, including isolation and restricted deployment. The decision is a rare admission that a model’s offensive potential justified a deliberate stop, and it underscores how much faster frontier cyber capabilities are advancing than their governance. Security teams should watch how these controls evolve, because they will shape the environment for AI-assisted vulnerability research and exploit development. OpenAI's Next AI Model Astra Shows Cyber Performance Strong Enough to Trigger Pause
