Actively exploited

The lead story is a reminder that the window between patch and exploitation is now measured in days, not months. A maximum-severity remote code execution vulnerability in SAP Commerce Cloud, patched just three days prior, is already being actively targeted in attacks, according to threat intelligence firm Defused Max severity SAP Commerce Cloud flaw now targeted in attacks. For organizations running large commerce deployments, this is not a routine update cycle: the flaw is severe enough to warrant emergency prioritization, and the presence of active attacks means unpatched systems should be treated as compromised until proven otherwise. Enterprise teams should verify patching status immediately, check for indicators of post-exploitation activity, and consider isolating Commerce Cloud instances that cannot be updated right away. The speed of exploitation suggests attackers are closely monitoring vendor advisories and weaponizing within days, which makes delayed patch deployment an unacceptable risk for internet-facing commerce infrastructure.

Also under active attack is a macOS Screen Sharing authentication bypass. The Netherlands’ national cyber security centre warns that attackers are exploiting the flaw after public exploit code became available, and observed campaigns are deploying Monero miners on compromised Macs Hackers exploit macOS Screen Sharing flaw to deploy Monero miner. The use of a cryptocurrency miner rather than ransomware or spyware is notable: it signals low-barrier, mass exploitation, likely automated scanning of exposed Screen Sharing services. The availability of public exploit code removes any doubt about attacker capability, and the targeted service is one that many organizations leave enabled for remote administration. Defenders should disable Screen Sharing where it is not strictly required, restrict access by firewalls or VPNs, and check for unexpected processes associated with mining activity. This is also a useful reminder that macOS is not immune to commodity exploitation, especially when authentication bypasses are involved.

Breaches

France has confirmed unauthorized access to systems at the Directorate General of Public Finances, with the intrusion traced to the theft or misuse of someone’s identity France investigates tax authority breach after hacker claims 600,000 victims. A hacker claims 600,000 victims, which would make this a significant breach of state-held personal data. The confirmation of unauthorized access matters, but the scale and the exact data involved remain unclear. Tax authority databases are high-value targets because they contain identity documents, financial details, addresses, and other data useful for fraud and social engineering. Even if the claimed victim count is inflated, the potential exposure of a large French government dataset warrants serious concern. Individuals affected may face targeted phishing or identity fraud, and the French authorities’ handling of notification and remediation will be under scrutiny. For other agencies, this incident underscores the importance of protecting administrative accounts, since credential theft or misuse appears to be the initial vector.

In a separate financial-sector incident, four cybercriminals were arrested in Brazil and three others were charged in Europe over an alleged €30 million bank fraud scheme Hackers arrested over €30M bank fraud exploiting service provider flaw. The scheme exploited a vulnerability at a service provider, allowing the criminals to withdraw funds from Commerzbank customers’ accounts. This is a supply-chain attack in the truest sense: the bank itself was not necessarily the direct target, but the compromise of a trusted provider gave attackers a path to customer funds. The arrests and charges are a positive enforcement outcome, but the underlying lesson is uncomfortable for financial institutions that rely on third-party service providers. Due diligence on a provider’s security posture is not enough; banks need continuous assurance, contractual rights to audit, and visibility into the provider’s access paths to customer accounts. The €30 million figure also illustrates the payout potential of compromising a single intermediary, which explains why such providers are increasingly attractive targets.

The third breach story highlights how a single third-party provider can create cascading risk across multiple public-sector organizations. A Scottish government agency, specifically the prosecutor’s office, reported a breach originating from a shared third-party provider that may have serviced other agencies as well Scottish Govt Suffers Potentially Widening Data Breach at Prosecutor's Office. The phrase “may have serviced other agencies” is the key concern: this could widen into a multi-agency data breach if the provider’s environment was broadly accessible. Public-sector bodies often share common suppliers for IT, payroll, or records management, and a compromise in that shared layer can quickly move from one agency's incident to a systemic one. The Scottish government will need to determine the provider’s full client list, the data shared with it, and whether the attacker had lateral access beyond the initially affected agency. This story should push other public bodies using shared service providers to ask whether they actually know where their data flows and whether they would be notified if a supplier were breached.

Industry

Finally, there is a notable policy development: NIST is exploring whether AI can help address the rising tide of vulnerability reports, driven in part by AI-augmented scanning and research Amid AI-Driven Bug-Hunt Tsunami, NIST Looks to … AI. The vulnerability management ecosystem is effectively being buried by the same automation that makes finding bugs cheaper and faster. NIST’s consideration of AI assistance is a recognition that human-only analysis may no longer keep pace with the volume of incoming vulnerability data. For security teams, this matters on two levels. First, the quality of vulnerability feeds and CVE enrichment depends on the ability of bodies like NIST to process submissions quickly and accurately, so any AI assistance could improve or complicate that pipeline. Second, the trend toward AI-driven discovery will continue to increase the number of vulnerabilities that organizations must triage, making automated prioritization and contextual risk scoring essential. NIST’s move is a pragmatic acknowledgment that defenders must use the same automation advantages as attackers, but it also raises questions about accuracy, false positives, and the risk of automating judgment in a field where context matters. Security teams should watch this development as a signal of where vulnerability management is heading.
