Federal agencies are under a three-day order to patch an actively exploited Zimbra flaw, while a critical Keycloak bug could let unauthenticated attackers take over any account. The day's reporting also covers a UK power plant shut down by Iran-linked hackers, Android car systems turned into a proxy botnet, and a major financial firm caught in a targeted breach campaign.

Actively exploited

CISA's three-day patching mandate for Zimbra Collaboration Suite CISA orders urgent patching of actively exploited Zimbra flaw is the clearest signal yet that this is not a routine vulnerability notification. The agency has ordered U.S. government agencies to remediate an actively exploited ZCS flaw within three days, and while the order formally applies to federal networks, any organization running Zimbra should treat it as an immediate threat. The vulnerability puts email at direct risk, and active exploitation means attackers already have a working path into affected servers. CISA does not use that window lightly. Even for organizations outside federal scope, the mandate is a useful benchmark: if the bug is urgent enough for a three-day order, private-sector follow-through should happen on a similar timescale. If you have Zimbra in your environment, this is a stop-everything-and-patch moment.

Vulnerabilities

Red Hat and the Keycloak project have patched a critical flaw in the identity and access management server, assigned CVE-2026-18963 Critical Keycloak Password Reset Flaw Could Let Unauthenticated Attackers Take Over Any Account. The vulnerability, rated 9.1 on the CVSS scale, lives in the reset-credentials flow of the keycloak-services component. An unauthenticated attacker can force a password reset for any user without requiring the email verification link, effectively letting the attacker set new credentials and take over the account. The flaw is a weak password-recovery mechanism (CWE-640), and the impact is the worst case for an IAM system: full account compromise from the internet, without authentication and with no user interaction. The CVE is not in CISA's Known Exploited Vulnerabilities catalog; that is not evidence that exploitation has not occurred. For organizations using Keycloak as their identity provider, this is effectively a master-key compromise. Deployments should be treated as a must-patch, especially if exposed or supporting privileged accounts.

Breaches

The most consequential incident in this section is the attack on a UK power plant by Iran-linked hackers Iran-Linked Hackers Shut Down UK Power Plant for Four Days, which shut down operations for four days. This was not a data exfiltration exercise; it crossed into real-world operational disruption. The reporting raises concerns about the resilience of Britain's distributed energy infrastructure and the possibility of repeatable attacks. For anyone responsible for critical infrastructure, this is a reminder that attackers are willing to cross the threshold from theft to physical impact, and that smaller distributed generation assets may be softer targets than large central plants.

Apollo Global Personal Information Exposed in Apollo Global Data Breach disclosed a data breach that exposed personal information at the private equity firm. The company appears to have been targeted as part of a campaign focusing on major financial companies, which adds a worrying pattern to what might otherwise be treated as an isolated incident. Financial firms should assume that targeted intrusion campaigns are working through a list of high-value institutions.

Also in the breach column, researchers have spotted malware infecting Android-based car systems and conscripting them into a proxy botnet Hackers infecting Android car systems to build proxy botnet. The vehicles become network nodes for relaying traffic, expanding the botnet economy to a class of connected devices that is not typically patched with the rigor of a server fleet. The long-term risk is that cars, like routers and cameras before them, become a permanent part of the attack surface.

Research

Operation QUICSILVER Operation QUICSILVER Targets Myanmar Government and IT with QUICAgent Backdoor is a China-nexus espionage campaign targeting Myanmar's government and IT sectors. The attackers use graduation ceremony invitation lures to deliver a Go backdoor called QUICAgent. Researchers assess with moderate confidence that the activity is the work of a China-nexus actor, and the targeting pattern suggests a deliberate collection effort against state and technology infrastructure rather than spray-and-pray malware distribution. The use of Go for the backdoor makes cross-platform deployment easier and complicates signature-based detection.

UAT-10147 UAT-10147 Uses AI to Scale Server Attacks, Deploys SPECTRE With EDR Bypass and Linux Rootkit shows how AI is beginning to change the economics of server-side attacks. The Chinese-speaking cybercrime group is targeting Windows and Linux web servers across education, media, technology, and gaming, with most victims in Brazil, Bolivia, China, Canada, and Vietnam. The group deploys a toolset called SPECTRE that includes an EDR bypass and a Linux rootkit, and is using AI to scale attack operations. This is a useful reminder that AI-enabled attacks are not a future concern; they are already widening the blast radius of commodity intrusion campaigns.

Industry

TikTok has reached a $400 million settlement with the U.S. Justice Department over children's privacy TikTok Reaches $400 Million Settlement With US Justice Department Over Children’s Privacy, paying $300 million immediately and another $100 million after an order vacates the earlier consent decree against Musical.ly. The settlement is the latest sign that children's privacy enforcement is moving from warning letters to real financial consequences. For platforms that collect data on minors, the message is that children's privacy obligations are being enforced with the full weight of the federal government.
